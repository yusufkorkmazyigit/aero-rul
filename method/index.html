    Method¶

    Data¶

    N-CMAPSS (Arias Chao et al., 2021) simulates a fleet of turbofan engines flying until failure under real flight conditions. Each subset is an HDF5 file with a development split and a test split. Every row is one second of flight:

    Group Content
    W flight conditions: altitude, Mach number, throttle resolver angle, fan inlet temperature
    X_s 14 physical sensors: temperatures, pressures, shaft speeds, fuel flow
    X_v virtual sensors estimated by the engine model
    T health parameters, the simulator's hidden degradation state
    A engine id, flight cycle, flight class, health state
    Y remaining useful life in flight cycles (the label)

    We use DS02, the subset most often used for data-driven prognostics: six development engines (2, 5, 10, 16, 18, 20) and three test engines (11, 14, 15).

    From seconds to flights¶

    Degradation moves from one flight to the next, not from one second to the next. Each flight cycle becomes one row: for every signal in W and X_s we take the mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum over the flight, plus the flight class and its length. This removes sensor noise and turns millions of rows into a few hundred per engine.

    The file is read one engine at a time, so memory use stays at the size of one engine.

    History features¶

    One flight is noisy: weather, payload and route change every reading. Wear shows as a trend. For every per-flight mean and maximum we add the trailing mean and least-squares slope over the engine's last ten flights (fewer at the start of its life). Only the current and earlier flights of the same engine enter a row; a test checks that changing a later flight leaves every earlier row unchanged.

    What the model may not see¶

    Column Why it is excluded
    unit an identifier, not a property of the engine
    cycle with a fixed end of life, the flight count nearly gives away the answer
    hs derived from the simulator's hidden degradation
    T group the hidden degradation itself

    Evaluation¶

    Consecutive flights of one engine are nearly identical. A random split of rows would put neighbours of every test row into training and report an error the model will never reach on a new engine. We therefore:

    1. cross-validate with leave-one-engine-out on the six development engines, and
    2. report the final score on the three test engines, which no training step touches.

    Every number is a mean and standard deviation over five seeds.

    Metrics¶

    RMSE and MAE are in flight cycles. The NASA scoring function (Saxena et al., 2008) penalises late predictions harder, because predicting more remaining life than there is moves maintenance past the failure. With \(d = \hat{y} - y\):

    \[ s = \sum_i \begin{cases} e^{-d_i/13} - 1 & d_i < 0 \\ e^{d_i/10} - 1 & d_i \ge 0 \end{cases} \]

    Ten cycles late costs about 1.72; ten cycles early about 1.16. Lower is better.

    Prediction intervals¶

    A planner needs a range and a statement of how often such ranges are right. We build intervals several ways and measure coverage on the test engines.

    Split conformal. Hold out two development engines for calibration and train on the other four. With absolute calibration errors \(r_1, \dots, r_n\), the radius is the \(\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil\)-th smallest error, and the interval is \(\hat{y} \pm q\). If calibration and test points are exchangeable, coverage is at least \(1-\alpha\) for any model.

    Cross-conformal. Take the errors from leave-one-engine-out predictions, so every error comes from a model that never saw that engine, and train the final model on all development engines. It uses the data better; its formal guarantee is weaker, which is one more reason to measure coverage directly.

    Quantile regression. LightGBM fit to the \(\alpha/2\) and \(1-\alpha/2\) quantiles. Widths adapt to each input, but nothing guarantees coverage.

    Conformalized quantile regression (Romano, Patterson and Candès, 2019). The score of a calibration point is how far its true value lies outside the quantile interval, \(E = \max(\hat{q}_{lo} - y,\; y - \hat{q}_{hi})\); the conformal quantile \(q\) of those scores widens (or narrows) the interval to \([\hat{q}_{lo} - q,\; \hat{q}_{hi} + q]\). Scores come from leave-one-engine-out predictions.

    Normalised conformal (Lei et al., 2018). Divide each leave-one-engine-out error by a difficulty estimate \(\hat{\sigma}(x)\), take the conformal quantile \(q\) of \(|y - \hat{y}| / \hat{\sigma}(x)\), and return \(\hat{y} \pm q\,\hat{\sigma}(x)\). The guarantee is the same as for cross-conformal, but widths follow the estimated difficulty. Two choices of \(\hat{\sigma}\): a straight line in the predicted RUL (norm_pred, two parameters) and a small LightGBM on the features (norm_feat). The difficulty model for each development engine's score is itself fitted without that engine, and \(\hat{\sigma}\) is floored at a tenth of the mean error so that no point gets a near-zero interval.

    Exchangeability

    Test engines 14 and 15 fly a different mix of flights from the training engines. If coverage falls short on them, that is evidence of distribution shift, and we report it as a finding rather than tune it away.

    Maintenance decisions¶

    An interval is only useful if it improves a decision. The decision modelled here: after every flight, book the engine into the shop \(L\) flights ahead (lead time, default 10) as soon as a statistic \(s_t\) of the prediction satisfies \(s_t \le L\). With true remaining life \(r_t\) at the booking flight, the engine fails if \(r_t < L\) and otherwise wastes \(r_t - L\) flights. If nothing triggers, it fails. Cost per engine: waste \(+\,R \times\) failure, with \(R\) the number of wasted flights one unplanned failure is worth, swept from 20 to 200.

    Policies differ only in \(s_t\): the point estimate; the point estimate minus a margin tuned on out-of-fold predictions of the training engines for each \(R\); and the lower edge of each 90 % interval. Cross-conformal's lower edge is \(\hat{y} - q\), itself a constant margin, chosen by a coverage target instead of a cost.

    Three test engines would give three decisions. The study therefore holds out every engine of DS02 in turn and builds the models, intervals and margins from the remaining eight, with an inner leave-one-engine-out wherever calibration needs it.

    Hyperparameter search benchmark¶

    We compare three optimisers for LightGBM: the canonical Artificial Bee Colony algorithm (Karaboga, 2005) through abcopt, random search, and Optuna's Tree-structured Parzen Estimator. Everything except the optimiser is fixed: the same seven-parameter search space, the same budget of candidate evaluations, the same leave-one-engine-out objective, five seeds.

    abcopt counts every proposal against the budget, including a configuration it has already tried; repeats are served from a cache and cost no compute. If anything this is strict on ABC.

    The chosen configuration is refit on all development engines and scored once on the test engines. A better CV score that does not carry over to the test engines means the search overfit the validation engines.

    Explanations¶

    LightGBM computes exact TreeSHAP values. We report mean |SHAP| on the test engines, summed per physical signal. SHAP explains the model, not the engine: correlated sensors share credit, and a high value means the model relies on a signal, not that the signal causes degradation.

    Limitations¶

    • Three test engines are few; results are reported per engine as well as pooled.
    • N-CMAPSS is a high-fidelity simulation, not fleet data.
    • Normalised conformal intervals adapt their width but, on DS02, lose coverage near end of life; the default interval therefore has constant width.
